# About Me

Hello, I’m **Jarel Shahim** — welcome to my portfolio. My journey into engineering has been anything but straightforward, shaped by family traditions, blue-collar work, leadership in unexpected places, and a steady drive to turn hands-on experience into technical mastery.  

## My Journey  

I grew up around machines and problem-solving. My grandfather ran a transportation business, and my earliest lessons in engineering came from crawling under trucks with my dad — pulling transmissions, dropping axles, or scraping asphalt from belly dumps. Those moments weren’t glamorous, but they built in me a resilience and practicality that I carry into every project today.  

In high school, I explored machining, welding, and CAD evently becoming a part of **VICA (Vocational Industrial Clubs of America)**, while also tinkering with computers in an after-school club. I learned early on that building — whether it’s a physical machine or a messy video game — was my way of making sense of the world.  

After graduation, I headed to the **oilfields of North Dakota**. The work was grueling and conditions unforgiving, but it taught me resourcefulness. I learned how to improvise, keep systems running, and stay composed in high-stakes environments.  

My path also took a turn into food service. At **Chipotle**, I rose from line cook to **Service and Kitchen Manager**, leading teams through high-volume operations. Managing people under pressure taught me leadership, communication, and adaptability — qualities I now bring to engineering projects just as readily as I once brought them to a Friday night dinner rush. Later, at **McMenamins**, I kept that balance of teamwork and precision alive in another fast-paced kitchen setting.  

When I returned to pursue my **B.S. in Mechanical Engineering at Oregon State University**, I knew balancing school and work would be part of the journey. Over those years, I supported myself through jobs at **Chipotle, Lowes Resaw & Specialty Cuts, and McMenamins**, each at different points in my college career. Those experiences became as much a part of my education as the classroom.  

At Chipotle, the challenge wasn’t just the dinner rush — it was learning how to stay calm when everything sped up, how to manage a team when the pressure was on, and how to carry responsibility for others. At Lowes, the long hours with heavy machinery taught me that efficiency and safety aren’t abstract ideas — they’re what keep people productive and injuries from happening. Later, at McMenamins, balancing late shifts with early classes pushed me to become more disciplined with my time than I ever thought possible.  

Juggling these jobs with engineering coursework meant I often went from a lecture on fluid mechanics straight into a shift on the line, or from an evening working in the shop into a night of CAD modeling. It wasn’t easy, but it gave me a perspective that many of my peers didn’t have: that **engineering isn’t just theory, it’s about showing up consistently, working with others, and finding a way forward even when you’re exhausted**. Those years built resilience, sharpened my focus, and made me appreciate the value of every opportunity I earned.  

As I progressed through school, I gravitated toward **thermo-fluid dynamics, combustion, and heat transfer** because they echoed the same questions I used to ask in the shop or on the oilfields: *why do systems fail under stress, and how can we make them run more efficiently?* Courses in **stress and fatigue analysis** deepened that perspective, teaching me how small design choices ripple into long-term reliability. At the same time, projects in **manufacturing techniques** tied classroom concepts back to the realities I had already lived — handling materials, operating machines, and understanding how safety and efficiency are built into process design.  

As my studies advanced, I built a toolbox that let me bridge theory with application: **CAD/CAM for design iteration, finite element analysis (FEA) for validation, DFMA for practicality, and MATLAB for modeling complex systems**. That foundation came to life during my role as **Lead Mechanical Designer on a NASA-sponsored capstone project supporting the Psyche Mission**. There, I applied **thermal, structural, and electrical analyses** to evaluate material behavior under extreme aerospace conditions, using **CAD and additive manufacturing** to prototype and test electromechanical subsystems. In that project, the skills I had been developing weren’t just academic exercises — they became critical to delivering designs that balanced innovation with feasibility under real mission constraints.  

More recently, I’ve extended that foundation into **robotics and electromechanical integration**, tying together my interest in dynamic systems with modern control and automation. Each step of my journey has reinforced the same lesson: engineering works best when rigorous analysis, practical design, and hands-on prototyping converge to solve real problems.  

## What I Value  

At the core of my work are a few guiding principles:  

- **Resourcefulness:** From oilfields to kitchens to fabrication shops, I’ve learned to adapt, find solutions, and keep things moving forward.  
- **Collaboration:** Some of my best work has come from listening, sharing, and building alongside others — whether cross-disciplinary teammates on a NASA project or coworkers in a machine shop.  
- **Clarity and accessibility:** I believe good engineering is about making complex systems understandable — whether for teammates, clients, or end users.  
- **Continuous growth:** Every job I’ve held — cooking, cutting pallets, debugging code — has been a stepping stone. I approach engineering with the same mindset: always learning, always improving.  

## My Approach  

I balance **technical rigor with practical experience**. I value precision in my calculations, but I never lose sight of manufacturability and real-world constraints. I thrive in team settings where ideas are tested and challenged, but I’m also comfortable taking initiative and pushing projects forward independently.  

I’ve come to see engineering as a conversation between design and reality: ideas meet prototypes, prototypes meet failure, and failure becomes the starting point for innovation. That’s where I feel most at home — in the space between concept and execution.  

## Beyond the Work  

This portfolio is a snapshot of my development as an engineer. It features **capstone and research-level work** like the NASA Psyche Mission project, but also **selected coursework, homework assignments, and mini-projects** from my time at Oregon State. These smaller projects — whether coding simulations in MATLAB, building CAD assemblies, or analyzing thermo-fluid systems — demonstrate not only my technical skills, but also the process of growth that’s brought me here.  

What ties it all together is my philosophy: engineering is about solving problems, building systems that work, and improving both the process and the outcome. I want this portfolio to give you more than a list of skills — I want it to show the story of how I got here, and where I’m headed.  

Thanks for visiting, and I’d love to connect if you’d like to collaborate, share ideas, or talk about the challenges that push us to think differently.
